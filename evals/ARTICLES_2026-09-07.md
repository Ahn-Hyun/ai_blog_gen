### The Transmission Chain: From Central Bank Policy to Consumer Reality

The chain begins with the Federal Reserve's primary lever: the federal funds rate. When the central bank initiates a rate cut, it lowers the cost for banks to borrow money overnight. This action is intended to signal a shift in macroeconomic policy, often aimed at stimulating economic activity during periods of uncertainty or slowing growth. However, this is where the direct control of the central bank ends.

The next link in the chain is the long-term Treasury market, specifically the 10-year Treasury yield, which serves as the foundational benchmark for long-duration assets. Unlike overnight rates, long-term Treasury yields are driven by forward-looking market expectations regarding inflation, fiscal stability, and overall economic growth. There is no fixed floor for future Treasury yields. If macroeconomic indicators suggest persistent inflation or fiscal instability, long-term Treasury yields can remain elevated or highly volatile, completely ignoring the reductions in short-term Fed rates.

From the Treasury baseline, the transmission moves into the secondary mortgage market, where individual home loans are bundled into mortgage-backed securities (MBS). Here, the mechanism becomes heavily dependent on investor sentiment and risk pricing. MBS investors do not simply accept the Treasury yield; they demand a premium to hold mortgage debt. This premium must account for the structural frictions of the market, including cash-flow differences, credit insurance, and the costs of intermediation.

Crucially, this stage of the transmission chain introduces the volatility premium. High rate volatility makes it exceedingly difficult to price long-duration assets. When the broader interest rate environment is unstable, lenders and secondary market participants are forced to build larger risk buffers into the rates they offer. If a Fed rate cut occurs during a period of high economic uncertainty, interest rate volatility often remains elevated across the broader bond market, prompting MBS investors to require higher compensation.

The final, and perhaps most critical, mechanism in this chain is the pricing of embedded option risk, specifically prepayment risk. When interest rates decline, homeowners hold the option to refinance their mortgages to secure lower monthly payments. For the MBS investor, this means their expected stream of long-term interest income could be abruptly cut short, forcing them to reinvest the returned principal into a lower-yielding market. Because this refinancing behavior is unpredictable, investors preemptively price in higher prepayment risks.

The ultimate market effect of this convoluted transmission chain is that the adjusted coupon spread—the true premium demanded by investors—widens. This widening acts as a shock absorber. It can entirely offset the downward pressure from a Fed rate cut, leaving consumer mortgage rates elevated despite looser monetary policy.

## The Transmission Chain: From Event to Mechanism to Market Effect

To understand why the underlying interest-rate risk for technology stocks has not been removed, investors must map the transmission chain from central bank rhetoric to equity valuation mechanics. The process begins with the event itself, moves through the mechanism of discount rate adjustments, and ultimately manifests in the market effect of valuation multiple compression.

### The Event: Conditional Policy Framing
The catalyst in this transmission chain is Anna Paulson’s May 2026 address. Rather than delivering a definitive policy roadmap, the speech was characterized by conditional framing. Paulson noted that while current policy is "mildly restrictive and appropriate," the future path of interest rates is not predetermined. This conditional stance is the critical first link in the chain. By explicitly stating that monetary policy might need to be tightened if persistent inflation expectations materialize, the event injected a sustained level of uncertainty into the macroeconomic outlook. The speech did not close the door on further restrictive action; instead, it wedged it open, ensuring that the threat of higher borrowing costs remains an active variable in market pricing.

### The Mechanism: Discount Rates and Long-Duration Assets
The second link in the transmission chain is the mechanism by which this conditional policy affects financial models. Technology companies are typically valued as long-duration assets. This means that a significant portion of their intrinsic value is derived from cash flows expected to be generated far into the future. In corporate finance and equity valuation, these future cash flows must be discounted back to their present value using a specific discount rate, which is heavily influenced by the risk-free rate set by the Federal Reserve.

When a central bank official preserves the option for further monetary restriction, the market must adjust its probability distribution for future interest rates. The absence of a hard rate ceiling means that the discount rate applied to tech sector cash flows cannot be safely capped. If the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) were to act on the hawkish contingencies outlined in Paulson's remarks, the risk-free rate would rise. Mechanically, as the discount rate increases, the present value of future cash flows decreases. This mathematical relationship is the core mechanism transmitting central bank optionality into equity risk.

### The Market Effect: Valuation Multiple Compression
The final link in the chain is the tangible market effect: multiple compression. Because technology stocks trade on long-duration cash flows that are highly sensitive to discount rates, any upward revision in rate expectations disproportionately impacts their valuations. Even if a technology company's underlying earnings performance remains stellar, a rising discount rate will mathematically compress the valuation multiples (such as the Price-to-Earnings or Enterprise Value-to-EBITDA ratios) that investors are willing to pay for that growth.

There is no empirical evidence in the May 2026 speech to suggest that tech-stock outperformance is guaranteed or that these equities are now insulated from broader monetary headwinds. If the FOMC is forced to resume tightening to combat persistent inflation expectations, the resulting liquidity drain would likely trigger a mechanical markdown of high-beta growth assets. Therefore, the transmission chain from Paulson's conditional remarks to the mathematical realities of discounted cash flow models confirms that structural interest-rate risk remains a dominant force in the market.
