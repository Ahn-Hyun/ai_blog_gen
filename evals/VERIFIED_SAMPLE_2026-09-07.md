Artificial intelligence is driving demand for new computing infrastructure, pushing data center construction costs higher. Standard per-megawatt construction metrics cover the physical building but exclude the tenant technology fit-out, local construction variables, and the financial impact of grid-connection delays.

According to [JLL Research](https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/market-outlook/data-center-outlook), the average global data center construction cost rose from $7.7 million per megawatt (MW) in 2020 to $10.7 million per MW in 2025. [JLL Research](https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/market-outlook/data-center-outlook) forecasts this average will reach $11.3 million per MW in 2026.

The $10.7 million figure for 2025 covers only the building's "shell and core." Outfitting a facility for high-density AI deployments requires separate capital expenditures. According to [JLL Research](https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/market-outlook/data-center-outlook), tenant technology fit-outs can cost up to $25 million per MW for advanced AI deployments. This upper-bound cost shows why shell-and-core metrics do not reflect the total AI project budget.

Global averages also omit local variables like labor, materials, and land costs. Capital planning must account for operational delays as well. Grid-connection waits in primary markets currently exceed four years on average, per [JLL Research](https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/market-outlook/data-center-outlook). As a result, shell-and-core capital may be deployed years before a facility can power tenant equipment.

Data center development is capital-intensive and subject to extended delays. Construction costs, fit-out requirements, and power delays mean that end-user demand for AI infrastructure does not guarantee investor cash flows or returns for real estate investment trusts (REITs). Financial models need to separate the cost of the physical real estate from the cost of equipping it, and apply local, risk-adjusted timelines.
